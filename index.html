Relay orbit (optional)
An assumed two-body Keplerian orbit with editable elements (default: about 12 hours, elongated, apoapsis over the south). It is an illustration, not any real mission’s orbit. Comms count as in view when the lander sees the relay above its horizon and the relay sees Earth, not hidden by the Moon. Perturbations (lunar gravity field, Earth, Sun) are not modelled.
Lander power (optional)
Simple hourly energy balance: a sun-tracking panel at full output whenever the Sun clears the horizon, a constant day load, a higher survival-heater load when the Sun is down, 90% charging efficiency and a full battery at touchdown. Dust loss and cold battery loss start at 0: set them from your lander’s data sheet (cold loss applies while the Sun is down). A flat panel produces power in proportion to the sine of the Sun’s elevation, which is tiny at the pole. The result appears in the cell details.

Data and resources
  • JPL Horizons (ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons): used. Sun and Earth positions from this page were checked against Horizons output at three sites (see accuracy below), plus the Horizons check panel above.
  • Moon Trek (trek.nasa.gov/moon) and Quickmap (quickmap.lroc.im-ldi.com): reference for terrain, not yet integrated. The Terrain panel accepts horizon profiles read from LRO LOLA topography; none are built in.
  • CSA Moon Exploration: context only. Canadian contributions to Artemis are part of the wider lunar programme this kind of planning tool supports.
Limitations and accuracy
  • Terrain is not built in. The tool accepts a horizon profile per site (azimuth to elevation), the form real LOLA topography takes once processed. Until real profiles are loaded, one horizon slider stands in, so results at the pole are a sensitivity study, not a prediction. Terrain is the most important factor for a real landing.
  • Approximate calculations. Sun and Earth positions come from standard ephemeris series (Meeus), including libration. Checked against JPL Horizons (DE441): the Sun at three sites (Chandrayaan-3 on 2023-08-23; Shackleton, 1–8 Dec 2026; Malapert, 15–22 Dec 2026) and Earth, with lunar-surface parallax, at two (Shackleton and Malapert). Elevation was within 0.04° and azimuth within 0.17° in every comparison. Apollo 11 (10.5°) agrees with the published landing sun angle (about 11°). The Horizons check panel above adds a further comparison with real Horizons output at lon 130°E, lat −88.9° for 1–8 Dec 2026 (largest differences 0.03° in elevation, under 0.15° in azimuth). Site coordinates are approximate.
  • Simplified geometry. The Sun (radius 0.27°) and Earth (0.95°) count as in view once their upper edge clears the horizon; the horizon test is simple and visibility is sampled every hour, so very short events can be missed.
  • Simple power and comms models. The power budget is an hourly energy balance with the assumptions listed above; dust loss and cold battery loss are user inputs that start at 0. The optional relay is an assumed two-body Keplerian orbit with editable elements, not any real mission’s orbit, and perturbations are not modelled.
  • Not for flight use. PolarClock is a planning and education tool, not for real mission operations.
